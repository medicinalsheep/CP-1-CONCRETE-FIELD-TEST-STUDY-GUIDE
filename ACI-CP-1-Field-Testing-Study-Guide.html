    Certification, exams, and the JTA

    Confirm current exam rules, fees, recertification, and passing scores with ACI or your Local Sponsoring Group. Policies change.

    What Grade I is for

    The credential shows you can sample fresh concrete and run the standard field tests used for acceptance work: temperature, slump, density/yield, air content, and making/curing specimens. Owners, producers, and labs look for it because the tests feed strength, air, and uniformity decisions.

    Two exams, both required

    • Written exam — closed-book knowledge of the seven methods, typically built from the published JTA. Expect emphasis on sequence, timings, equipment limits, calculations at a conceptual level, and “what makes a test invalid.”
    • Performance exam — you demonstrate the methods with equipment. You must satisfy the examiner on each required procedure. Notes and coaching are not a substitute for fluent hands.

    Why the JTA matters

    ACI publishes a Job Task Analysis that lists the knowledge and skills the exams are designed to sample. Study the JTA as a blueprint: if a task is listed, you should be able to do it without looking it up. This guide groups those public themes; it is not the JTA itself.

    Study habit that transfers to the floor For every step, ask: What happens to the result if I skip this? Timing, moisture loss, incomplete consolidation, and contaminated samples are the usual ways field data become fiction.

    Recertification mindset

    Plan as if the card expires on a five-year cycle and the standards on your shelf may be a newer year than the ones you first learned. Always test against the edition in force for your project and for the exam administration you booked.

    Field workflow — sampling → cylinders

    High-level original checklist for study and dry-runs. Not an ACI performance script. Check items as you rehearse; state stays on this device.

    1. Plan the compositeKnow which tests you will run and whether strength work needs the larger sample volume (commonly cited ~1 ft³ / 28 L — verify in current ASTM C172).
    2. Sample middle portionsTwo or more portions from the middle of discharge; skip start/end; protect from sun, wind, rain, wash water.
    3. Combine & remix on the clockFinish combining within the short composite window (commonly cited ~15 min — verify in current ASTM C172); remix until uniform.
    4. Temperature firstImmerse/cover the sensor (commonly cited ~3 in / 75 mm); wait for a stable reading; start inside the fast post-sample clock (commonly cited ~5 min — verify C1064 / C172).
    5. SlumpThree equal-volume layers, 25 rods each, vertical lift (commonly cited 5 ± 2 s), measure original center; discard shear/collapse and retest if still representative.
    6. Air (and/or density)Choose C231 vs C173 with intent; consolidate, strike off, de-air / roll as required; apply aggregate correction on pressure meters; still on the fast clock.
    7. Mold cylindersStart within the specimen window (commonly cited ~15 min after composite — verify C172 / C31); match layers and rod to mold size; identify specimens.
    8. Initial cure immediatelyMoisture protection + temperature band; record min/max; plan transport without jarring, freezing, or drying.
    ASTM C172 / C172M

    Sampling freshly mixed concrete

    If the sample is wrong, every test after it is theater.

    High-level educational outline only. Follow the current C172 procedure in your purchased standard and CP-1 workbook.

    Purpose & significance

    You are building a composite that represents the batch — not the wettest shovel, not the first chute-full, not a pile that has already segregated. Acceptance tests are only as honest as this step.

    Key equipment

    • Clean, non-absorbent receptacles and a remixing pan
    • Shovel / scoop appropriate to the discharge
    • Protection from sun, wind, rain, and contamination
    • Means to wet-sieve when a later test cannot accept the full aggregate size
    High-level step summary
    1. Plan the sample around the tests you will run (strength work needs a larger composite).
    2. Take two or more portions from the middle of discharge at spaced intervals; do not intercept the very first or last concrete.
    3. Keep elapsed time between first and last portion inside the short composite window.
    4. Protect the material while you combine it; remix the composite until it looks uniform.
    5. Start temperature, slump, and air promptly; start molding specimens on their slightly longer clock.
    6. If a test method cannot accept the maximum aggregate in the mix, wet-sieve as that method requires — then remix what you will actually test.
    Clocks that show up on exams and job sites Composite portions close together (commonly cited ~15 min — verify in current ASTM C172). Fresh-property tests almost immediately (commonly cited ~5 min — verify in current ASTM C172). Cylinders soon after (commonly cited ~15 min — verify in current ASTM C172 / C31).
    Common pitfalls Sampling a wheelbarrow that already sat in the sun; scooping off a pile; taking only one grab from the chute; forgetting to remix; starting cylinders after slump and air have eaten the whole clock; contaminating the pan with wash water or debris.
    ASTM C1064 / C1064M

    Temperature of freshly mixed concrete

    Temperature is both a specification item and the context for every other result.

    High-level educational outline only. Follow current C1064 and the CP-1 workbook.

    Purpose & significance

    Mix temperature affects slump loss, air, set, and later strength. Hot loads can be rejected; cold loads can be damaged by freezing. The reading must represent the concrete mass, not the pan or the air.

    Key equipment

    • A thermometer whose accuracy and range match the method (commonly cited: about ±1°F / 0.5°C)
    • Enough concrete so the sensor is buried and surrounded
    • A container when the placement itself cannot provide cover around the sensor
    High-level step summary
    1. Place the sensor so it is immersed the required depth and surrounded by concrete in all directions (commonly cited ~3 in / 75 mm — verify in current ASTM C1064).
    2. Keep the instrument in place long enough for equilibrium (commonly cited ~2–5 min — verify in current ASTM C1064) — not a drive-by glance.
    3. Read while the sensor is still in the concrete when the device allows it.
    4. Record to the method’s resolution (often nearest 1°F or 0.5°C) and finish inside the short post-sampling clock.
    Accuracy notes A precise-looking display is worthless if the probe is in a thin edge, a sun-baked crust, or a container smaller than the cover distance. Large aggregate may need a larger sample just so the sensor is truly surrounded.
    Common pitfalls Pulling the thermometer out to read it; using a kitchen gadget with unknown accuracy; measuring leftover mortar in the pan; starting the clock after you already ran slump.
    ASTM C143 / C143M

    Slump of hydraulic-cement concrete

    A timed geometry test. If the cone, layers, rod, or lift are wrong, the number is not slump.

    High-level educational outline only. Follow current C143 and the CP-1 workbook.

    Purpose & significance

    Slump is a consistency index used in the field as a batch check. It is not “workability” in the full rheology sense, but a change in slump often flags water, air, or delay problems.

    Key equipment

    • Frustum mold: 12 in height, 8 in base diameter, 4 in top diameter
    • Tamping rod: ⅝ in diameter, about 24 in long, hemispherical tip
    • Rigid, non-absorbent, level base
    • Scoop, ruler/scale readable to ¼ in (5 mm)
    High-level step summary
    1. Dampen the mold and base; do not leave standing water.
    2. Hold the mold firmly to the base. Fill in three layers of approximately equal volume.
    3. Rod each layer 25 times, distributing strokes. Penetrate into the layer below on the upper lifts.
    4. Strike off the top, clean the base, then lift the mold vertically in 5 ± 2 seconds with no twisting or tapping.
    5. Measure the vertical difference between the original mold height and the displaced original center of the top surface, to the nearest ¼ in (5 mm).
    6. Complete the test inside the short elapsed-time limit (commonly cited ~2½ min from the start of filling — verify in current ASTM C143).
    Invalid-test instinct If the concrete shears or falls away to one side instead of slumping symmetrically, discard and retest. Do not “average” a collapsed pile.
    Common pitfalls Equal-height layers instead of equal-volume; 25 taps instead of 25 rods; lifting too slowly; measuring the edge instead of the original center; running the test on a bouncing pickup tailgate; using a cone with dented dimensions.
    ASTM C138 / C138M

    Density (unit weight), yield, and gravimetric air

    A mass-and-volume test that tells you how much concrete the batch actually made.

    High-level educational outline only. Follow current C138 and the CP-1 workbook for formulas and reporting.

    Purpose & significance

    Density is the measured mass of concrete in a calibrated measure. From density you can compute yield (volume produced) and, with theoretical density, a gravimetric air content. It catches over-yield, under-yield, and air problems that a slump cone cannot see.

    Key equipment

    • Calibrated measure (bowl) of known volume
    • Balance/scale of suitable capacity and sensitivity
    • Tamping rod, mallet, strike-off plate
    • Vibrator when the method requires it for very low-slump concrete

    Relationships to remember (concepts, not a formula sheet to photocopy)

    • Density D = net mass of concrete / volume of the measure
    • Yield = total mass of batched materials / density (gives volume produced)
    • Relative yield compares actual yield with the design volume
    • Gravimetric air compares theoretical air-free density with measured density

    Practice the algebra in your official workbook until unit conversions (ft³ vs yd³, lb vs kg) are automatic.

    High-level step summary
    1. Confirm the measure’s volume is current (calibration is not a forever number).
    2. Fill in the required number of equal-volume layers for that measure size.
    3. Consolidate each layer (rod 25 times when rodding applies; tap the sides with a mallet the specified number of times).
    4. Strike off flush, clean all concrete from the exterior, and weigh.
    5. Compute density, then yield / air as required by the project and the method.
    Common pitfalls Weighing mortar stuck to the outside of the bowl; using an uncalibrated measure; skipping mallet taps; strike-off that leaves a mound or a hollow; mixing up batch weights (SSD vs as-batched water).
    ASTM C231 / C231M

    Air content by the pressure method

    The everyday Type B meter test — powerful, fast, and the wrong tool for porous lightweight aggregate.

    High-level educational outline only. Follow current C231, meter manufacturer instructions, and the CP-1 workbook.

    Purpose & significance

    Entrained air protects against freeze–thaw. Too little air risks durability; too much air costs strength. Pressure meters infer air from how the concrete compresses under a known pressure. They assume aggregate particles do not have a large, compressible pore system that would look like “air” on the gauge.

    Key equipment

    • Type A or Type B air meter (Type B is common in North American field work)
    • Calibration vessel / equipment required by the method — calibration is part of competence, not a once-in-a-career sticker
    • Rod, mallet, strike-off bar or plate, syringe/water bottle, clamps
    • Known aggregate correction factor for that mixture (and a habit of updating it when aggregate changes)

    Equipment limits (JTA angles)

    • Not appropriate for lightweight concrete or mixes with highly porous aggregate — use volumetric C173 instead.
    • Leaking lids, clogged petcocks, damaged bowls, or a gauge that will not hold initial pressure are invalid-test conditions waiting to happen.
    • Report to the method’s precision (commonly cited ~0.1% — verify in current ASTM C231).

    Aggregate correction (high level)

    Even some normalweight aggregates give a small pressure response of their own. The aggregate correction factor is determined for the mixture (or as the method describes) and subtracted so the reported value tracks the concrete’s air of interest — not aggregate pore “air.” A correction from last season’s gravel is not automatically valid for this week’s stone.

    Layering & consolidation

    Fill the bowl in the equal-volume layers the method specifies for that bowl size. Rod each layer the required strokes; tap the sides with a mallet the commonly cited ~10–15 times per layer idea — verify in current ASTM C231. Strike off flush: a mound or hollow changes the known volume the percentage is based on.

    High-level step summary
    1. Confirm the meter is appropriate for this aggregate (if porous/lightweight → stop and use C173).
    2. Fill the bowl in equal layers; rod and tap as the method specifies; strike off flush; clean the flange.
    3. Clamp the cover. On Type B meters, use the petcocks and water to remove trapped air from the lid space until discharge runs clear/bubble-free as the method requires.
    4. Pump or adjust to the initial pressure, close valves in the required order, and release the chamber into the bowl.
    5. Tap and wait for a stable gauge reading — a swinging needle is not a result.
    6. Apply the aggregate correction factor. Report to the method’s precision.
    Invalid-test & edge-case instincts (study themes)
    • Using C231 on lightweight or highly porous aggregate mixes.
    • Skipping petcock water purge so lid-space bubbles become fake “air.”
    • Reading before the gauge settles; leaking clamps; dirty sealing surfaces.
    • Omitting or misapplying the aggregate correction.
    • Over-consolidating so violently that you knock out entrained air you meant to measure.
    • Starting so late that the composite has already aged past the fresh-test clock (commonly cited ~5 min after composite — verify in current ASTM C172).
    Must-do JTA ideas Know when C231 is the wrong method (lightweight, slag, highly porous particles → volumetric C173). Know that calibration and aggregate correction are part of the test, not optional extras. Know that a Type B lid must be de-aired with water through the petcocks.
    Common pitfalls Dirty flanges that leak; skipping water through the petcocks; using last month’s correction factor on a new aggregate; reading the gauge before it settles; consolidating so hard that entrained air leaves; reporting more precision than the method supports.
    ASTM C173 / C173M

    Air content by the volumetric method

    The roll-a-meter. Slower than pressure, and the right tool when aggregate pores would fool C231.

    High-level educational outline only. Follow current C173 and the CP-1 workbook.

    Purpose & significance

    The volumetric method displaces the concrete’s air with water (and isopropyl alcohol to kill foam) inside a calibrated neck. Because it does not assume incompressible aggregate, it is the field choice for lightweight and other porous particles — and it remains valid on many normalweight mixes when specified.

    Key equipment

    • Calibrated bowl + top section with graduated neck (volumetric air meter)
    • Isopropyl alcohol at the concentration the method expects (commonly discussed as ~70%) and calibrated cups/dispensers — guessing pours is not a procedure
    • Rod, mallet, strike-off, funnel, watertight cap
    • Enough rolling space and a level place to stand the meter for readings

    Equipment / process limits

    • Leaking cap or worn gasket → liquid loss → meaningless neck line.
    • Insufficient rolling/inversion leaves a plug of undisturbed concrete — that is an invalid test, not a “close enough” reading.
    • Foam that never clears hides the meniscus; alcohol additions are measured, not improvised.
    • Consecutive readings must agree within the method’s tolerance (commonly cited ~0.25% — verify in current ASTM C173).

    C231 vs C173 — choose with intent

    Pressure (C231)Volumetric (C173)
    Normalweight, dense aggregateUsualAllowed
    Lightweight / porousNot appropriateThe field method
    SpeedFasterMore inversion, rolling, standing
    Foam / alcoholNot the mechanismCentral to a valid reading
    Aggregate correctionRequired themeDifferent mechanism — not the same correction ritual
    High-level step summary
    1. Fill the bowl in the specified layers (commonly two equal-volume layers — verify in current ASTM C173); rod and tap; strike off.
    2. Attach the top section. Add water, then the initial measured alcohol, and cap watertight.
    3. Invert and shake, then roll, using the method’s minimum times so the concrete actually moves through the meter.
    4. Stand upright, let foam settle, and read the neck. If foam is excessive, add measured alcohol as the method allows and keep track of additions that affect the calculation.
    5. Repeat rolling/standing until consecutive readings agree within the method’s tolerance.
    6. Disassemble and inspect: a plug of undisturbed concrete means air was not displaced — the test is invalid. Start over with remaining representative concrete if still on the clock.
    Invalid-test & edge-case instincts (study themes)
    • Weak rolling that never breaks the concrete mass; skipping the post-test dump inspection.
    • Guessing alcohol volume; stopping at the first foamy glance.
    • Averaging two disagreeing neck readings instead of continuing the method’s repeat rules.
    • Leaking meter or lost liquid during inversion.
    • Trying to “save time” by switching a lightweight mix to C231 mid-job.
    Timing note C173 takes longer than a Type B shot. Plan the composite so you can still finish air inside the fresh-test window (commonly cited ~5 min after composite for starting fresh tests — verify in current ASTM C172) or prioritize sequence with the inspector before the load arrives.
    Common pitfalls Weak rolling; unmeasured alcohol; first foamy reading as “the answer”; skipping the dump inspection; reporting more precision than the neck supports; starting so late the sample is no longer representative.
    ASTM C31 / C31M

    Making and curing test specimens in the field

    Cylinders are not souvenirs. Early temperature and moisture write the strength story.

    High-level educational outline only. C31 is revised often. Follow the current standard and CP-1 workbook for temperatures, demolding, and transport windows.

    Purpose & significance

    Field-made specimens estimate potential strength of the concrete as delivered — but only if molding, initial curing, transport, and final lab curing follow the method. A perfect slump test cannot rescue cylinders that froze, dried, or baked in a truck cab.

    Key equipment

    • Molds (commonly 6×12 in or 4×8 in cylinders) that hold dimension and do not leak
    • Matching tamping rod (larger rod for 6×12, smaller for 4×8)
    • Mallet, strike-off tools, vibration equipment if the method requires it for that consistency
    • Initial-curing setup with temperature control and moisture protection, plus a min/max thermometer
    • Transport containers that cushion and prevent moisture loss / freezing

    Layering & consolidation (must-know)

    • 6×12 in: commonly 3 equal layers, ⅝-in rod, 25 strokes per layer — verify in current ASTM C31
    • 4×8 in: commonly 2 equal layers, ⅜-in rod, 25 strokes per layer — verify in current ASTM C31
    • Tap sides after rodding; strike off; finish the top so it can be capped/ground later without fiction
    • Vibrate only when the method says that consistency calls for vibration — not “to be extra sure” on a high-slump mix

    Initial curing temperature bands (study level)

    Protect from moisture loss immediately. Temperature must stay in the band required for the specified strength class. A commonly cited study band for many ordinary strengths sits near ~60–80°F; high-strength work is often discussed with a tighter, cooler-centered band — verify the exact limits in current ASTM C31. Record min/max; a noon glance does not prove the overnight story.

    Transport & lab curing

    After the initial-curing period, move specimens without jarring, freezing, or drying. Transport is often discussed in a roughly ~24–48 h envelope after molding — verify in current ASTM C31. Final lab curing is moist and tightly temperature-controlled; it answers a different question than field initial curing when projects use both field-cured and standard-cured sets.

    High-level step summary
    1. Start molding inside the specimen clock after the composite is made (commonly cited ~15 min — verify in current ASTM C172 / C31).
    2. Place molds on a level, rigid surface away from vibration and traffic.
    3. Fill in the specified number of equal layers for that mold; rod the specified strokes with the matching rod; tap the sides; strike off and finish the top.
    4. Identify each specimen so it cannot be confused later.
    5. Move immediately into initial curing: prevent moisture loss and hold temperature in the required band; start the min/max record.
    6. After the initial-curing period, transport carefully; then final-cure in the laboratory environment required by the method.
    Invalid-test & edge-case instincts (study themes)
    • Wrong layer count or wrong rod for the mold size.
    • Moving soft cylinders; molds on a bouncing truck bed while still plastic.
    • Open tops that dry; “curing box” that is just a cooler with no temperature check.
    • Initial curing on a hot steel deck, in a closed cab, or next to a heater.
    • Vibrating high-slump concrete that the method says to rod (or the reverse).
    • Late molding after the composite aged out; unlabeled twins that get swapped at the lab.
    Curing is part of the test Acceptance cylinders that sat on a hot steel deck, in an open truck bed, or next to a space heater are not C31 specimens. Know the difference between initial field curing and standard lab curing — they answer different questions when a project uses both.
    Common pitfalls Using the ⅝-in rod in a 4×8 mold; two layers in a 6×12; moving soft cylinders; no lid / no wet burlap so the top dries; “curing box” with no min/max record; vibrating when you should rod.
