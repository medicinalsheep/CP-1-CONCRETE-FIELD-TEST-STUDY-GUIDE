What Grade I is for

The credential shows you can sample fresh concrete and run the standard field tests used for acceptance work: temperature, slump, density/yield, air content, and making/curing specimens. Owners, producers, and labs look for it because the tests feed strength, air, and uniformity decisions.

Two exams, both required

  • Written exam — closed-book knowledge of the seven methods, typically built from the published JTA. Expect emphasis on sequence, timings, equipment limits, calculations at a conceptual level, and “what makes a test invalid.”
  • Performance exam — you demonstrate the methods with equipment. You must satisfy the examiner on each required procedure. Notes and coaching are not a substitute for fluent hands.

Why the JTA matters

ACI publishes a Job Task Analysis that lists the knowledge and skills the exams are designed to sample. Study the JTA as a blueprint: if a task is listed, you should be able to do it without looking it up. This guide groups those public themes; it is not the JTA itself.

Study habit that transfers to the floor For every step, ask: What happens to the result if I skip this? Timing, moisture loss, incomplete consolidation, and contaminated samples are the usual ways field data become fiction.

Recertification mindset

Plan as if the card expires on a five-year cycle and the standards on your shelf may be a newer year than the ones you first learned. Always test against the edition in force for your project and for the exam administration you booked.

ASTM C172 / C172M

Sampling freshly mixed concrete

If the sample is wrong, every test after it is theater.

High-level educational outline only. Follow the current C172 procedure in your purchased standard and CP-1 workbook.

Purpose & significance

You are building a composite that represents the batch — not the wettest shovel, not the first chute-full, not a pile that has already segregated. Acceptance tests are only as honest as this step.

Key equipment

  • Clean, non-absorbent receptacles and a remixing pan
  • Shovel / scoop appropriate to the discharge
  • Protection from sun, wind, rain, and contamination
  • Means to wet-sieve when a later test cannot accept the full aggregate size
High-level step summary
  1. Plan the sample around the tests you will run (strength work needs a larger composite).
  2. Take two or more portions from the middle of discharge at spaced intervals; do not intercept the very first or last concrete.
  3. Keep elapsed time between first and last portion inside the short composite window.
  4. Protect the material while you combine it; remix the composite until it looks uniform.
  5. Start temperature, slump, and air promptly; start molding specimens on their slightly longer clock.
  6. If a test method cannot accept the maximum aggregate in the mix, wet-sieve as that method requires — then remix what you will actually test.
Clocks that show up on exams and job sites Composite portions close together (about 15 min). Fresh-property tests almost immediately (about 5 min). Cylinders soon after (about 15 min). Confirm the current wording in C172.
Common pitfalls Sampling a wheelbarrow that already sat in the sun; scooping off a pile; taking only one grab from the chute; forgetting to remix; starting cylinders after slump and air have eaten the whole clock; contaminating the pan with wash water or debris.
ASTM C1064 / C1064M

Temperature of freshly mixed concrete

Temperature is both a specification item and the context for every other result.

High-level educational outline only. Follow current C1064 and the CP-1 workbook.

Purpose & significance

Mix temperature affects slump loss, air, set, and later strength. Hot loads can be rejected; cold loads can be damaged by freezing. The reading must represent the concrete mass, not the pan or the air.

Key equipment

  • A thermometer whose accuracy and range match the method (commonly cited: about ±1°F / 0.5°C)
  • Enough concrete so the sensor is buried and surrounded
  • A container when the placement itself cannot provide cover around the sensor
High-level step summary
  1. Place the sensor so it is immersed the required depth and surrounded by concrete in all directions (commonly taught as about 3 in / 75 mm).
  2. Keep the instrument in place long enough for equilibrium — typically a few minutes, not a drive-by glance.
  3. Read while the sensor is still in the concrete when the device allows it.
  4. Record to the method’s resolution (often nearest 1°F or 0.5°C) and finish inside the short post-sampling clock.
Accuracy notes A precise-looking display is worthless if the probe is in a thin edge, a sun-baked crust, or a container smaller than the cover distance. Large aggregate may need a larger sample just so the sensor is truly surrounded.
Common pitfalls Pulling the thermometer out to read it; using a kitchen gadget with unknown accuracy; measuring leftover mortar in the pan; starting the clock after you already ran slump.
ASTM C143 / C143M

Slump of hydraulic-cement concrete

A timed geometry test. If the cone, layers, rod, or lift are wrong, the number is not slump.

High-level educational outline only. Follow current C143 and the CP-1 workbook.

Purpose & significance

Slump is a consistency index used in the field as a batch check. It is not “workability” in the full rheology sense, but a change in slump often flags water, air, or delay problems.

Key equipment

  • Frustum mold: 12 in height, 8 in base diameter, 4 in top diameter
  • Tamping rod: ⅝ in diameter, about 24 in long, hemispherical tip
  • Rigid, non-absorbent, level base
  • Scoop, ruler/scale readable to ¼ in (5 mm)
High-level step summary
  1. Dampen the mold and base; do not leave standing water.
  2. Hold the mold firmly to the base. Fill in three layers of approximately equal volume.
  3. Rod each layer 25 times, distributing strokes. Penetrate into the layer below on the upper lifts.
  4. Strike off the top, clean the base, then lift the mold vertically in 5 ± 2 seconds with no twisting or tapping.
  5. Measure the vertical difference between the original mold height and the displaced original center of the top surface, to the nearest ¼ in (5 mm).
  6. Complete the test inside the short elapsed-time limit (commonly 2½ minutes from the start of filling).
Invalid-test instinct If the concrete shears or falls away to one side instead of slumping symmetrically, discard and retest. Do not “average” a collapsed pile.
Common pitfalls Equal-height layers instead of equal-volume; 25 taps instead of 25 rods; lifting too slowly; measuring the edge instead of the original center; running the test on a bouncing pickup tailgate; using a cone with dented dimensions.
ASTM C138 / C138M

Density (unit weight), yield, and gravimetric air

A mass-and-volume test that tells you how much concrete the batch actually made.

High-level educational outline only. Follow current C138 and the CP-1 workbook for formulas and reporting.

Purpose & significance

Density is the measured mass of concrete in a calibrated measure. From density you can compute yield (volume produced) and, with theoretical density, a gravimetric air content. It catches over-yield, under-yield, and air problems that a slump cone cannot see.

Key equipment

  • Calibrated measure (bowl) of known volume
  • Balance/scale of suitable capacity and sensitivity
  • Tamping rod, mallet, strike-off plate
  • Vibrator when the method requires it for very low-slump concrete

Relationships to remember (concepts, not a formula sheet to photocopy)

  • Density D = net mass of concrete / volume of the measure
  • Yield = total mass of batched materials / density (gives volume produced)
  • Relative yield compares actual yield with the design volume
  • Gravimetric air compares theoretical air-free density with measured density

Practice the algebra in your official workbook until unit conversions (ft³ vs yd³, lb vs kg) are automatic.

High-level step summary
  1. Confirm the measure’s volume is current (calibration is not a forever number).
  2. Fill in the required number of equal-volume layers for that measure size.
  3. Consolidate each layer (rod 25 times when rodding applies; tap the sides with a mallet the specified number of times).
  4. Strike off flush, clean all concrete from the exterior, and weigh.
  5. Compute density, then yield / air as required by the project and the method.
Common pitfalls Weighing mortar stuck to the outside of the bowl; using an uncalibrated measure; skipping mallet taps; strike-off that leaves a mound or a hollow; mixing up batch weights (SSD vs as-batched water).
ASTM C231 / C231M

Air content by the pressure method

The everyday Type B meter test — powerful, fast, and the wrong tool for porous lightweight aggregate.

High-level educational outline only. Follow current C231, meter manufacturer instructions, and the CP-1 workbook.

Purpose & significance

Entrained air protects against freeze–thaw. Too little air risks durability; too much air costs strength. Pressure meters infer air from how the concrete compresses. They assume aggregate particles do not have a large, compressible pore system.

Key equipment

  • Type A or Type B air meter (Type B is common in North American field work)
  • Calibration vessel / equipment required by the method
  • Rod, mallet, strike-off bar or plate, syringe/water bottle, clamps
  • Known aggregate correction factor for that mixture
High-level step summary
  1. Fill the bowl in equal layers; rod and tap as the method specifies; strike off flush.
  2. Clean the flange, clamp the cover, and use the petcocks/water to remove trapped air from the lid space on Type B meters.
  3. Pump or adjust to the initial pressure, close valves in the required order, and release the chamber into the bowl.
  4. Tap and wait for a stable gauge reading.
  5. Apply the aggregate correction factor. Report to the method’s precision (often 0.1%).
Must-do JTA ideas Know when C231 is the wrong method (lightweight, slag, highly porous particles → volumetric C173). Know that calibration and aggregate correction are part of the test, not optional extras.
Common pitfalls Dirty lid bowls that leak; skipping water through the petcocks; using last month’s correction factor on a new aggregate; reading the gauge before it settles; consolidating so violently that you knock out entrained air.
ASTM C173 / C173M

Air content by the volumetric method

The roll-a-meter. Slower than pressure, and the right tool when aggregate pores would fool C231.

High-level educational outline only. Follow current C173 and the CP-1 workbook.

Purpose & significance

The volumetric method displaces the concrete’s air with water (and isopropyl alcohol to kill foam) inside a calibrated neck. Because it does not assume incompressible aggregate, it is the field choice for lightweight and other porous particles.

Key equipment

  • Calibrated bowl + neck (volumetric air meter)
  • 70% isopropyl alcohol and calibrated cups/dispensers
  • Rod, mallet, strike-off, funnel, watertight cap

C231 vs C173 — choose with intent

Pressure (C231)Volumetric (C173)
Normalweight, dense aggregateUsualAllowed
Lightweight / porousNot appropriateThe field method
SpeedFasterMore inversion, rolling, standing
Foam / alcoholNot the mechanismCentral to a valid reading
High-level step summary
  1. Fill the bowl in the specified layers (commonly two), rod and tap, strike off.
  2. Attach the top section. Add water, then the initial measured alcohol, and cap watertight.
  3. Invert and shake, then roll, using the method’s minimum times so the concrete actually moves.
  4. Stand the meter upright, let foam settle, and read the neck. If foam is excessive, add measured alcohol as the method allows.
  5. Repeat rolling/standing until consecutive readings agree within the method’s tolerance (often 0.25%).
  6. Disassemble and inspect: a plug of undisturbed concrete means the test did not displace air — it is invalid.
Common pitfalls Weak rolling that never breaks the concrete mass; guessing alcohol instead of measuring it; stopping at the first foamy reading; skipping the post-test dump inspection; reporting more precision than the neck can support.
ASTM C31 / C31M

Making and curing test specimens in the field

Cylinders are not souvenirs. Early temperature and moisture write the strength story.

High-level educational outline only. C31 is revised often. Follow the current standard and CP-1 workbook for temperatures, demolding, and transport windows.

Purpose & significance

Field-made specimens estimate potential strength of the concrete as delivered — but only if molding, initial curing, transport, and final lab curing follow the method. A perfect slump test cannot rescue cylinders that froze, dried, or baked in a truck cab.

Key equipment

  • Molds (commonly 6×12 in or 4×8 in cylinders) that hold dimension and do not leak
  • Matching tamping rod (larger rod for 6×12, smaller for 4×8)
  • Mallet, strike-off tools, vibration equipment if required
  • Initial-curing setup with temperature control and moisture protection, plus a min/max thermometer
  • Transport containers that cushion and prevent moisture loss / freezing
High-level step summary
  1. Place molds on a level, rigid surface away from vibration and traffic.
  2. Fill in the specified number of equal layers; rod the specified strokes; tap the sides; strike off and finish the top.
  3. Identify each specimen so it cannot be confused later.
  4. Move immediately into initial curing: prevent moisture loss and hold temperature in the band required for that specified strength (commonly a wider band for ordinary strengths and a tighter, cooler-centered band for high-strength concrete).
  5. Record curing temperatures (min/max is a frequent JTA theme).
  6. After the initial-curing period, transport without jarring, freezing, or drying; then final-cure in the laboratory environment required by the method (moist, tightly controlled temperature).
Curing is part of the test Acceptance cylinders that sat on a hot steel deck, in an open truck bed, or next to a space heater are not C31 specimens. Know the difference between initial field curing and standard lab curing — they answer different questions when a project uses both.
Common pitfalls Using the ⅝-in rod in a 4×8 mold; two layers in a 6×12; moving soft cylinders; no lid / no wet burlap so the top dries; “curing box” that is just a cooler with no temperature check; vibrating high-slump concrete that should have been rodded (or the reverse).
